  <h2>RICO. Pattern of Racketeering. <br/>Psychological and Legal Warfare of Organized Crime</h2>

  <p>Organized criminal enterprises that operate through or capture parts of the justice system (sometimes called “state-embedded” or “judicial-police racketeering”) tend to rely on a recognizable set of psychological and procedural tactics. These patterns appear repeatedly in documented public-corruption cases, RICO prosecutions, and academic studies of organized crime.</p>

<div class="glass-section">
  <h2 id="legal-procedural-tactics">2. Legal / Procedural Tactics (Abuse of Process)</h2>
  
  <p>
  These groups prefer to use legitimate-looking legal tools rather than purely street-level violence:
</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Weaponized restraining orders / protective orders</strong> – used to seize contact with children, exclude a person from property, or create a criminal exposure if the order is violated.</li>
  <li><strong>Selective or retaliatory prosecution</strong> – criminal charges timed to coincide with civil disputes or public complaints.</li>
  <li><strong>Denial of access to court</strong> – vexatious-litigant findings, repeated continuances, refusal to calendar motions, or procedural barriers that make it nearly impossible to be heard.</li>
  <li><strong>Asset freezes, wage garnishments, and QDROs</strong> – formal legal mechanisms used to extract money under color of law.</li>
  <li><strong>Control of information</strong> – restricted access to body-camera footage, sealed records, or selective disclosure.</li>
  <li><strong>Forum shopping and capture of key decision-makers</strong> – keeping cases before friendly judges or officials.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<div class="glass-section">
  <h2 id="extortion-and-seizure">3. Extortion and Direct Seizure of Assets</h2>

  <p>
  Organized criminal groups usually prefer ongoing extortion (regular payments or concessions) because it is lower-risk and generates continuous income. They typically switch to direct seizure or theft when:
  </p>

  <ul>
    <li>The victim becomes too resistant or publicly exposes the scheme.</li>
    <li>The victim’s assets are large and liquid enough to justify the risk (retirement accounts, real estate, businesses).</li>
    <li>The group believes it has sufficient control of the relevant institutions that the seizure will be difficult to reverse.</li>
    <li>External pressure (federal scrutiny, media, or a change in political environment) makes continued quiet extortion riskier than a quick grab.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

<div class="glass-section">
  <h2 id="recurring-mindset">4. Recurring Mindset and Behavioral Patterns</h2>

  <p>
  Documented characteristics of these enterprises include:
</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>View of the justice system as a tool, not a constraint</strong> – rules are followed only when useful.</li>
  <li><strong>Long time horizons</strong> – willingness to play multi-year games of attrition.</li>
  <li><strong>Information asymmetry</strong> – heavy investment in knowing the victim’s finances, family situation, psychological pressure points, and legal vulnerabilities.</li>
  <li><strong>Layered protection</strong> – use of official positions, procedural complexity, and mutual protection among participants so that no single actor appears fully responsible.</li>
  <li><strong>Retaliation as deterrence</strong> – visible punishment of those who resist or complain, to discourage others.</li>
  <li><strong>Narrative control</strong> – aggressive effort to control how the story is told to outsiders (courts, media, oversight bodies).</li>
</ul>

<div class="glass-section">
  <h2 id="rico-pattern-identification">5. How a RICO / racketeering pattern is identified</h2>

  <p>
  These are observed patterns from historical and contemporary public-corruption and RICO cases. <br/>
  When such patterns appear, they are usually investigated as public corruption, honest-services fraud, extortion under color of law (18 U.S.C. § 242), or RICO violations.
  </p>


<br/>

<p>
  <strong>Under 18 U.S.C. §§ 1961–1968, a civil or criminal RICO theory generally requires:</strong>
</p>

<ul>
  <li>An enterprise (association-in-fact of persons or entities)</li>
  <li>That affects interstate commerce</li>
  <li>A pattern of racketeering activity — at least two predicate acts within 10 years, related and continuous</li>
  <li>Conduct or participation in the enterprise’s affairs through that pattern</li>
  <li>Injury to business or property (civil RICO)</li>
</ul>

<br/>

<p>
  <strong>Common predicates in public-corruption cases:</strong>
</p>

<ul>
  <li>Extortion / Hobbs Act — 18 U.S.C. § 1951</li>
  <li>Honest-services fraud — 18 U.S.C. §§ 1341, 1343, 1346</li>
  <li>Deprivation of rights under color of law — 18 U.S.C. § 242 / civil 42 U.S.C. § 1983</li>
  <li>Conspiracy against rights — 18 U.S.C. § 241</li>
  <li>Obstruction of justice — 18 U.S.C. §§ 1503, 1512</li>
  <li>Money laundering / monetary transactions — 18 U.S.C. §§ 1956–1957</li>
  <li>Mail/wire fraud in furtherance of the scheme</li>
</ul>

<br/>

<p>
  <strong>Pattern indicators courts actually look for:</strong>
</p>

<ul>
  <li>Same actors, same playbook, repeated over time</li>
  <li>Official process used to extract money, documents, or silence</li>
  <li>Retaliation after complaints or disqualification filings</li>
  <li>Concealment of conflicts or disqualifications</li>
  <li>Parallel civil and criminal pressure timed to coerce settlement</li>
</ul>

<div class="glass-section">
  <h2 id="resist-institutional-capture">8. How to resist statewide / institutional capture (lawful)</h2>
  
  <p>
  <strong>Priorities, in order:</strong>
</p>

<ul>
  <li>Get out of the captured forum — removal, venue transfer, federal stay</li>
  <li>Stop the most dangerous process — warrants, garnishments, 401(k) QDRO, pending charge</li>
  <li>Preserve evidence — copies off-site, litigation holds, redacted public filings</li>
  <li>Create an official federal record — not only websites and social media</li>
  <li>Income and safety — lawful work, housing, do not violate stay-away orders</li>
  <li>Oversight referrals — FBI/DOJ/U.S. Attorney with a short evidence index</li>
</ul>

<p>
  You cannot “prosecute” judges yourself. You can document, remove, stay, and refer. Keep a clean federal docket.
</p>

<p>
  <strong>California (void / recusal / process)</strong>
</p>

<ul>
  <li>CCP §§ 170–170.6 (disqualification; acts after disqualification)</li>
  <li>CCP § 170.4(d) — acts after disqualification are void</li>
  <li>CCP § 391 et seq. (vexatious litigant — you argue the order is void)</li>
  <li>Penal Code §§ 1004, 1385 (demurrer / dismissal in furtherance of justice)</li>
  <li>Family Code / DVPA provisions on restraining orders (void if issued without jurisdiction)</li>
</ul>

<p>
  <strong>Texas (once the case is in WD Texas)</strong>
</p>

<ul>
  <li>Federal procedure controls the removed case</li>
  <li>Texas disciplinary / AG oversight is for Texas actors, not California judges</li>
  <li>Protective orders and stays issued by the federal court are the operative shield</li>
</ul>

<p>
  <strong>Voidness concepts courts recognize</strong>
</p>

<ul>
  <li>No jurisdiction after removal (§ 1446(d))</li>
  <li>No jurisdiction after effective disqualification (CCP § 170.4(d))</li>
  <li>Fraud on the court (narrow; requires clear, specific proof)</li>
  <li>Orders obtained without notice/due process may be void or voidable</li>
</ul>

<p>
  Courts often void post-disqualification and post-removal acts.
